Syllabus

Course description

This course introduces quantitative research methods to study questions from international relations and European politics, using modern tools for scientific programming and reproducible research. You will learn to analyse and visualise data in R, document your work in Quarto, and manage your projects with Git and GitHub. Together these tools support state-of-the-art workflows for transparent, collaborative and well-organised empirical research. All work is done in Positron, an integrated development environment for data science.

In this course, we will learn the regression paradigm as a general framework for modeling patterns in data – essentially, fitting lines and curves to describe relationships among variables – and Bayesian inference as a framework for quantifying uncertainty and learning efficiently from data.

Course objectives

The overall goal is to give you the practical skills and the methodological confidence to write an empirical bachelor thesis in political science. You will learn to preprocess data, choose and implement appropriate models for continuous and binary outcomes, compute quantities of interest, and interpret results substantively.

Beyond technical proficiency, the course emphasises sound statistical reasoning, effective data visualisation, and communicating empirical findings clearly to a non-technical audience. Through weekly assignments and a cumulative take-home exam, you will develop the ability to evaluate statistical claims critically in academic research and in public debate alike.

Course logistics

General information

The course meets on Wednesdays, 13:45-15:15, in A5, 6 – C 108. Each session consists of an instructor-led part on concepts and theory, followed by hands-on exercises. Homework is assigned at the end of class and is due 7 days later, before the next session. Readings provide background, theory and context, and are linked per week from the Schedule.

Tip

If you have to miss a session, tell me beforehand. Email me before the session and I will set up a Zoom room so you can follow it remotely.

Prerequisites

Two different things go by this name, and it is worth keeping them apart. The first is an administrative condition on taking the module. The second is what you need on day one to follow what happens in the room.

Formally. The module requires the Basismodul Einführung in die Internationalen Beziehungen and the Basismodule in Methoden und Statistik. Whether you meet those is a question for the module catalogue and the Studienbüro, not for me.

In terms of what this course assumes: nothing. No programming experience is assumed, and no statistics beyond descriptive measures. Everything the course needs, it teaches.

A basic grasp of statistics does help, and if you would like to shore that up before or during the term, Section 3.12 points at two free ways to do it.

Module, credits and workload

  • Module: Advanced Module: International Relations
  • This course: Methods exercise class (Übung)
  • Credits: 6 ECTS, which is 180 hours of total workload
  • Recommended semester: 5th Semester
  • Contact time: 19.5 hours, that is 13 sessions of 90 minutes
  • Self-study: 160.5 hours, about 12.3 hours a week across the 13 teaching weeks

That self-study figure is not decoration. It is where the weekly homework, the readings and your exam preparation actually live, and the course is paced on the assumption that you spend roughly that much time on it.

For comparison, the module catalogue budgets a nominal 30 hours of contact time and 150 hours of self-study for a 6 ECTS course. The difference is simply that this course meets for 90 minutes over 13 weeks rather than for the nominal figure, so a little more of the same total lands outside the room.

The authoritative version of all of these is the module catalogue and the examination regulations for the B.A. Political Science, not this page. Where the two disagree, the regulations win.

Attendance policy

Active participation (Mitarbeit) is a required component of the coursework (Studienleistung), on the basis of section 13b of the examination regulations. This course consists of 13 sessions. Successful participation is presumed if you attend at least 80% of sessions, that is at least 11 out of 13.

If attendance falls below 80% but stays at or above 60% (8 to 10 out of 13), successful participation is not presumed. In that case the possibility of fulfilling the participation requirement is assessed case by case.

If attendance falls below 60% (fewer than 8 out of 13), successful participation is normally considered impossible under the examination regulations.

If sessions are cancelled during the semester, the absolute number of required sessions is adjusted accordingly, while the percentage thresholds stay unchanged.

Grading policy

Under the examination regulations, assessment consists of coursework (Studienleistung, pass or fail) and a final examination (Prüfungsleistung, graded).

Successful completion of all coursework requirements is a prerequisite for admission to the final take-home exam.

Coursework (Studienleistung, pass or fail)

Coursework has two components, and both must be passed. This is the model the module catalogue provides for rather than a local invention: it names Mitarbeit and Hausaufgaben among the Studienleistungen for this course.

1. Weekly homework assignments. There are 11 homework assignments over the term, one in every session except the first and the last. The first session is orientation and the last is synthesis and the exam briefing, so neither sets work, which leaves weeks 2 to 12. They are distributed through GitHub and assessed pass or fail. To fulfil this requirement you must pass at least 80% of them, that is 9 out of 11. Homework must show a serious and complete attempt at the tasks. A submission that is empty or clearly insufficient does not count as passed.

2. Active participation (Mitarbeit). Active participation, as described in Section 3.4, is a required coursework component and is assessed pass or fail.

The take-home exam

The final examination (Prüfungsleistung) is a take-home exam, and it determines the whole course grade. In the module catalogue it goes by its formal name, a written term paper (schriftliche Hausarbeit). Those are two names for the same thing.

Window: 9 December 2026, 15:30 to 23 December 2026, 23:59.

It is distributed through the exam repository on the course GitHub organisation, the same template flow as the weekly homework. The last version pushed to your exam repository before the deadline is what gets graded. Late submissions are not accepted.

What you receive. A dataset, the theoretical background you need, and a small number of stated hypotheses. You do not have to find a topic, a question or data of your own.

What you produce. A short empirical research report that tests those hypotheses using the eight-step Bayesian workflow in Section 6. The rendered PDF follows the structure of an empirical political science paper: a data and methods (or research design) section, a results (or analysis) section, a discussion including limitations, and a brief conclusion. No introduction and no literature review are required, because the background and hypotheses are given to you.

  • The report may not exceed 6,000 words. That limit is set by the module catalogue, not by me. The reference list, the appendices and the statutory declaration do not count towards it, which is my clarification rather than the catalogue’s.
  • Use figures and tables wherever they genuinely support the argument.
  • Do not print raw code, warnings, messages or unformatted R output in the PDF.
  • Appendices are welcome for diagnostics, robustness checks and supporting material that is not essential to the main text.

What you submit.

  • firstname_lastname_exam.qmd and the rendered firstname_lastname_exam.pdf, at the root of your exam repository.
  • The whole repository, which is part of the submission. Any further notebooks or scripts are inspected and count towards the grade. Treat it as a research project.
  • A signed statutory declaration on the final page of the PDF, following the university’s template. An electronic signature is fine. A PDF without one cannot be graded.

How it is graded. On the university scale, 1.0 (very good) to 5.0 (insufficient), across the four dimensions in Table 1. The full criterion-by-criterion scheme, which is what you should actually work from, is distributed together with the exam.

Important

A submission that does not render from a clean clone cannot reach top marks. The single largest criterion in the code and repository dimension is that the PDF rebuilds from your repository on another machine. Test that before the deadline, not after.

Every tool and workflow the exam needs is taught during the course. Session 12, the multilevel primer, is the one exception: it is not examined.

Registration and withdrawal

Registration for the Prüfungsleistung, deregistration, and the applicable deadlines are handled by the Studienbüro under the examination regulations, not by me. Check the dates there and keep to them, because responsibility for meeting them sits with you.

If a good reason, illness in particular, prevents you from completing the exam within the window, section 30 of the examination regulations applies. You must submit a written application to the Studienbüro without delay, with evidence. The examination board decides, and the burden of proof is on the applicant. Tell me as well, but telling me is not a substitute for the formal application.

Section 5 additionally allows the deadlines for coursework and examinations to be extended on a timely written application, for instance for students with children or with dependants requiring care.

Disability and chronic illness

If a disability or a chronic illness makes it harder for you to take an assessment in the form it is set, you are entitled to compensation that restores equal opportunity, under section 6 of the examination regulations. Applications are made in writing and in good time, and applications on grounds of disability or chronic illness go to the university’s representative for students with disabilities or chronic illness.

Tip

Talk to me early, and tell me only what you want to. The formal route runs through the representative and the examination board, and I do not need to know the nature of your condition. What helps me is knowing early that an adjustment is coming, so it can be built in rather than improvised.

Language and software policy

The course is taught entirely in English and is built around R, Quarto and Git or GitHub for data analysis, reproducible research and project management. Work with these tools, because the exercises and workflows are designed around them.

For the take-home exam you may use another script-based language such as Python or Stata, but this is not recommended and no guidance is provided for non-R workflows. The emphasis is on understanding the tools, practising them, and learning to find solutions independently.

Hardware

You need a computer available during class. A standard laptop is sufficient, and there are no particular requirements for RAM, an external GPU, or other advanced components.

If you do not own a suitable device, the University of Mannheim IT department offers a laptop rental service. Please make use of it if you need it.

Software

Install all required software before the first session, following the separate installation guide. Doing it early means we can sort out any problems on day one and start the programming work properly in the following session.

Homework and feedback policy

A sample solution is published for every homework. Individual feedback is NOT provided by default. The only individual feedback available is the optional AI feedback you can opt into by adding an open-source license.md to your submission repo.

What that means in practice. A sample solution is published for every homework after its due date, and it is the standard against which you should read your own work. Individual comments on individual submissions are not part of this course by default.

Opting in to AI feedback. If you add an open-source license.md to your submission repository, you opt in to automated, individualised feedback. An AI reads your submission alongside the sample solution and writes a FEEDBACK.qmd and FEEDBACK.pdf back into your repository, organised by the eight workflow steps. No license.md means no processing at all. This is entirely optional, and opting out costs you nothing beyond the individual feedback itself.

Late work policy

Late submissions are not accepted. If you anticipate any difficulty in meeting a deadline, discuss it with me before the deadline, not after.

AI policy

All submissions are checked for plagiarism, and violations of the code of conduct may result in failing the course or in expulsion.

AI is encouraged

You will use AI tools during this course. That is a prediction, not a guess, and pretending otherwise would produce a policy nobody follows. So the question here is not whether but how to make AI use visible and trustworthy.

Tip

Use it, and think of it as a tutor. Always available, infinitely patient, and no question is too basic. Lean on it when you are stuck, to explain a concept, to tidy code, to improve your writing. You take final responsibility for everything you submit.

Two honest caveats. First, AI is more reliable on formal logic, programming and well-covered general topics than on narrow substantive questions in international relations, so treat narrow factual claims sceptically and check the source. Second, leaning on it before you understand the material does hinder learning: research suggests AI helps most those who already understand the topic. For a fuller explanation of why AI can undermine learning, see this piece by Andrew Heiss. Do the thinking first, then let the tool help.

Say so in the commit message

This course asks for one thing in return, and it is a habit rather than a paperwork exercise.

Important

When a commit contains work an AI helped with, say so in the commit message. That is the whole rule. You do not have to separate your work from AI-assisted work into different commits, and you do not have to submit transcripts.

This is the convention open-source software development already uses, so you are learning a real practice rather than a classroom one. The canonical form is a trailer line at the end of the message:

Tighten the interpretation of the interaction term

Co-Authored-By: Claude <noreply@anthropic.com>

A plain sentence in the message body does the same job, and is just as acceptable:

Rewrite the prior justification

Wording tidied up with AI. The choice of prior and the argument for it are mine.

Short messages can simply name it: Fix the join, AI helped debug it or Reformat code with AI. Say what the assistance did where that is not obvious, and be proportionate. A whole section drafted with AI is worth a sentence. A spell check is worth four words.

Why this instead of a chat transcript

A blanket declaration that “generative AI was used to improve the language of this manuscript” cannot be checked by anyone. It is a claim about a process that left no trace. Submitting chat transcripts does not work either, because a transcript cannot be matched to the passages it actually shaped.

Version control solves this, and you are learning it anyway. Your repository already records every change, who made it, when, and exactly what changed. Marking where AI entered turns that ordinary record into the disclosure, produced at the moment the work happens rather than reconstructed weeks later. It is honest about how mixed this work really is, because a sentence you drafted and a model tightened belongs to neither pile, and it rests on the same voluntary integrity that scientific standards have always rested on.

Where the line is. Asking AI to explain a concept, proofread your draft, clean up code you wrote, or help debug an error message: all fine, all encouraged, all easy to attribute. Generating an analysis from scratch and submitting it as your own, or systematically hiding AI involvement so the record shows none: not fine. The common thread is the same in both directions. The history must not misrepresent how the work came about.

Important

You do not need to know any of this yet. Git, commits and commit messages are taught in session 2, and you will practise this convention in every homework from then on. By the time the exam arrives it is a habit rather than a rule to memorise. Nobody is expected to arrive already knowing how to do it.

Note

Two different things, both involving AI. The policy above is about AI you use while doing your own work, which is disclosed in your commit messages. The opt-in feedback described in Section 4 is different: it is run by me, on an explicit license.md opt-in, and it comments on work you have already submitted. Using the feedback pipeline is never something you need to declare.

The backbone: from research question to empirical answer

Everything in this course builds toward one skill: getting from a question about the world to an answer you can defend, with its uncertainty attached. That route has eight steps, shown in Table 2, and they are the same eight steps every time. The take-home exam is exactly this route, run end to end on a dataset you meet at the end of the term.

Several of the words in that table probably mean nothing to you yet. That is expected, and it is the honest starting point. Estimand, prior predictive check, R-hat: learning what these are, why each step exists, and what goes wrong when you skip one, is what the term is for. You see the whole route in session 1, before you can walk any of it, so that every later session has somewhere to attach itself. By session 13 you will have run all eight steps yourself, more than once.

The Glossary holds the short version of every term, and each session’s slides hold the long version.

Session plan

What each session covers, and what you should be able to do afterwards. Dates, slides, homework and readings are on the Schedule.

Note

This is a preliminary plan and may be revised or updated throughout the semester.

Session 1: Introduction

09 September 2026 · workflow steps 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8

An orientation session. We fill in a short welcome survey together, then work out what statistics is doing at its core: counting, and asking how much a count could have varied by chance. The survey answers become a dataset of our own, and we use it for a first glance at Bayesian inference, running a whole analysis end to end before taking any single step apart. We close on the normal distribution, the one piece of notation that analysis leaned on throughout.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Say what statistics is doing at its core: counting, reasoning about how much a count could have varied by chance, and telling signal from noise.
  • Tell an estimand, an estimator and an estimate apart, and say what makes a claim an inference.
  • Get a first glance at Bayesian inference by following a worked analysis of a dataset we collected in the room.
  • Name the eight steps that analysis followed, which is also the shape of the take-home exam.
  • Read a normal distribution: what the mean and the standard deviation do to the curve, and where the 68 and 95 percent rule comes from.
  • Say how the course is structured, and find your way around the course site: schedule, syllabus, glossary and the homework flow.

Session 2: Reproducible literate programming with Quarto and Git

16 September 2026 · Scientific programming fundamentals

The working environment, and the habits that make a result checkable by somebody else. Everything happens in Positron from this session onwards.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Understand how Quarto, R, Git and GitHub fit together.
  • Create a project under version control and render a Quarto document.
  • Push your first reproducible document to GitHub, and create your own homework repository from a template, inside the course organisation.
  • Write a commit message a later reader can follow, which is also where AI assistance gets declared.

Session 3: R fundamentals: data structures, objects and functions

23 September 2026 · Scientific programming fundamentals

The language itself, and the small vocabulary everything later is assembled from. Time is spent deliberately on reading errors, because that is the skill that makes independent work possible.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Understand the core concepts: objects, functions and pipes.
  • Know the main data types R uses to store information.
  • Know the main data structures R uses to organise information.
  • Access and explore data using [] and $.
  • Read an error message and work out what R was actually asked to do.

Session 4: The tidyverse

30 September 2026 · Scientific programming fundamentals · workflow step 2

Getting data into a shape that tools can work with, which is most of the work in most analyses. Step 2 of the workflow, the data summary, starts here.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Understand what the tidyverse is and why it exists.
  • Know what tidy data means and why it matters.
  • Import data with readr and explore it with glimpse(), head() and summary().
  • Wrangle data with the core dplyr verbs, chained with the native pipe.
  • Use Quarto cell options for labels, captions and execution control.

Session 5: Data visualisation

07 October 2026 · Scientific programming fundamentals · workflow step 2

Moving from a plot that displays data to a figure that makes a point, plus the reshaping and joining that usually comes first. We close on probabilities and distributions, which is the bridge into the second block.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Reshape data between wide and long form with pivot_longer() and pivot_wider().
  • Combine datasets from several sources with left_join(), and verify the result.
  • Handle missing values transparently, and understand the risks of imputation.
  • Build and customise ggplot2 figures through aesthetics, geoms, scales and themes.
  • Produce publication-ready tables and embed computed results directly in prose with inline code.
  • Read a distribution as a statement about how plausible each value is.

Session 6: The logic of Bayesian inference

14 October 2026 · The logic of Bayesian inference · workflow steps 3, 4

The conceptual core of the course, and a deliberate change of gear: the programming gets easier and the thinking gets harder. Probability as a description of uncertainty, and the single rule that combines what you believed beforehand with what you observed.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Articulate the distinction between frequentist and Bayesian interpretations of probability, and explain why each suits different questions.
  • Explain why parameters are treated as uncertain quantities with distributions.
  • Name and interpret the three core ingredients: prior, likelihood and posterior.
  • State Bayes’ theorem in full, identify each term, and explain the role of the normalising constant.
  • Describe how the posterior changes as the prior changes and as data accumulates.
  • Apply grid approximation in R to compute and visualise a posterior.

Session 7: Markov chain Monte Carlo

21 October 2026 · The logic of Bayesian inference · workflow steps 4, 5, 6

The computational implementation of everything session 6 established. The emphasis is on reading the diagnostics rather than on deriving the algorithms.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Articulate why grid approximation is intractable for realistic models.
  • Explain the Monte Carlo idea: approximating integrals by random sampling and counting.
  • Define a Markov chain and explain what a stationary distribution is.
  • State the Metropolis acceptance rule and show why the normalising constant cancels.
  • Explain qualitatively how Hamiltonian Monte Carlo differs from random-walk Metropolis, and why it is more efficient.
  • Interpret brms output: trace plots, R-hat, effective sample size and posterior distributions.
  • Understand that convergence cannot be proven, only falsified, and recognise what non-convergence looks like.

Session 8: Bivariate linear regression

04 November 2026 · Applied modelling · workflow steps 1, 3, 4, 5, 6

The first full model, deliberately the simplest one: one outcome, one predictor, and the whole workflow run around it. Every later model extends this one rather than replacing it.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Frame a regression problem as estimand (what we want to know), estimator (the procedure) and estimate (the number we get).
  • Explain what OLS assumes, and see where the classical and the Bayesian account of the same model meet.
  • Specify weakly informative priors for regression coefficients and defend them on principled grounds.
  • Understand brms as sampling from the posterior: an estimator that returns a distribution rather than one number.
  • Interpret brms output and extract quantities of interest, such as predictions and marginal effects.

Session 9: Multivariate linear regression

11 November 2026 · Applied modelling · workflow steps 1, 7

More than one predictor, and the questions that come with it. A set of controls is a claim about how the world works, not a way of being safe.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Explain omitted variable bias, and use a DAG to classify a control variable as a confounder, a mediator or a collider.
  • Extend the Bayesian regression model to several predictors and interpret each coefficient as a partial effect.
  • Handle categorical predictors using dummy variables.
  • Compare competing models with Bayesian leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-IC).

Session 10: Interaction terms in linear regression

18 November 2026 · Applied modelling · workflow step 7

Effects that are not the same everywhere. Specification is the easy part, and interpretation is where interactions are usually got wrong, so most of the session goes there.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Specify a multiplicative interaction model and interpret the conditional marginal effect of a predictor as a function of its moderator.
  • Extract posterior marginal effects separately for each level of the moderator.
  • Compare an interaction model against the additive model with LOO-IC.
  • Show a conditional effect in a figure rather than asking a reader to combine coefficients.

Session 11: Modelling binary outcomes: logit and probit

25 November 2026 · Applied modelling · workflow steps 3, 7

Outcomes that are yes or no. Nothing about the engine changes here: we are only swapping the likelihood, and then doing the work of getting back to a scale a reader can use.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Explain why linear models are structurally wrong for binary outcomes, and how a Bernoulli likelihood plus a link function resolves it.
  • Fit and interpret a Bayesian logistic regression in brms, including priors on the logit scale and predicted probability ribbons.
  • Say how the probit link differs from the logit, and when the choice matters.
  • Assess model quality with posterior predictive checks, a confusion matrix, ROC and AUC, and compare models with LOO.

Session 12: Introduction to multilevel models

02 December 2026 · Applied modelling · workflow steps 3, 5, 7

Data with structure: candidates in parties, regions in countries, years in states. A primer and your departure point for later work, and the one session that is not on the take-home exam.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Identify hierarchical data and explain why treating grouped observations as independent understates uncertainty.
  • Describe the three-point spectrum of complete pooling, no pooling and partial pooling, and say when each is right.
  • Read the (1 | group) formula in brms and interpret the key output quantities.

Session 13: Beyond the course: applying what you have learned

09 December 2026 · Applied modelling · workflow steps 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8

Synthesis and outlook rather than new methods. We consolidate what you already have and hand you a clean runway into the exam.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Name what you learned across the three blocks, and why the course was built this way.
  • Read a classical regression table, since most of the literature you will meet reports one.
  • Use the tooling that makes the code and repository part of the exam grade straightforward.
  • Know exactly what the exam asks for, how it is graded, and how AI use is declared in it.
